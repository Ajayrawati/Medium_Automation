## Wikimedia Foundation Unionization Developments

On July 20, Wiki Workers United U.S. (WWU-U.S.) demanded voluntary recognition as a union from the Wikimedia Foundation (WMF) [6]. This followed a prior request for voluntary recognition made in June by WWU-UK, the UK branch of the union [6]. The U.S. group requested a response from the Wikimedia Foundation by Friday, July 24, which coincided with the Wikimania conference [6]. 

No response was received by the July 24 deadline, although the topic of Wikimedia staffers unionizing was discussed at the conference [6]. On July 27, the WMF published a statement declining voluntary recognition of Wiki Workers United U.S [6]. The statement noted the Foundation's belief that a secret-ballot election conducted by the National Labor Relations Board is the appropriate path forward, arguing that this process protects individual choice and reflects the collective will of participants [6]. 

The community response to the WMF statement has mostly been one of dismay [6]. Observers expressed fears that the Foundation might utilize the time gap leading up to a secret ballot to discourage employee involvement in union activities [6].

## Operating System and Software Milestones

In software development news, RISC OS Open marked its twentieth anniversary on June 20, 2006 [2]. Steve Revill noted that the organization was incorporated with the ambition of taking a proprietary operating system and opening it up to anyone interested in helping [2]. Over its first twenty years, the platform evolved significantly with community contributions, including code writing, bug filing, forum participation, and testing nightly builds [2]. Notable milestones included a shared source initiative announced with Castle in the autumn of 2006, the release of the first RISC OS sources into public hands by the Wakefield Show the following May, and earning a Drobe award for "Best show of initiative" during its second year [2].

Meanwhile, the general-purpose proof-oriented programming language F* continues its active development [3]. Developed by Microsoft Research, Inria, and the community, F* supports both purely functional and effectful programming, combining dependent types with proof automation based on SMT solving and tactic-based interactive theorem proving [3]. F* programs compile by default to OCaml, and various code fragments can be extracted to F#, to C or Wasm using KaRaMeL, or to assembly utilizing the Vale toolchain [3]. Distributed under the Apache 2.0 license, F* is implemented in itself and bootstrapped using OCaml [3].
